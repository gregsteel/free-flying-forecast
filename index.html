What the grade takes into account

  • Wind. Speed and direction at launch altitude (800 m2,625 ft), and for speed also the stronger of the lower winds (10 m and 850 hPa). The green direction is from NW (320 degrees) to NE (40 degrees). Speeds are averages. Hang gliders cope with more wind than paragliders, so the wind limits differ; everything else is the same.
  • Wind arrow. The triangle in each day shows where the launch wind is blowing: green inside the flyable direction, amber just outside it (marginally crossed: noted, but it does not lower the grade), red outside it.
  • Gusts. The model's forecast gust speed near the ground (about 10 m up) at its grid point, not at launch altitude, so the page shows it directly under the ground wind it goes with. Because that is not what a pilot meets at launch or in the air, the page also estimates the gust at launch and the gust at thermal height (marked est.). Gusts at launch of 14 kts26 kph or more make the block Bad (dangerous from 17 kts32 kph); so do gusts at thermal height of 24 kts45 kph or more (dangerous from 30 kts55 kph). Those are the paraglider limits; hang gliders cope with more gust as they do with more wind, so theirs are higher (see the Guide).
  • Sun. How much of the possible sunshine reaches the ground, shown under each hour: ☀️ sunny (70% or more), ⛅ some cloud shading, ☁️ heavily shaded (under 40%). Cloud shading weakens thermals: below 70% the thermal quality is scaled down in proportion, and below 40% the reasons say the day is heavily shaded.
  • Rain and storms. Rain rate, and storm energy (CAPE), which shows how likely thunderstorms and strong gust fronts are.
  • Thermals. Strength and height, which separate Good from Poor. Height and updraft are AUSRASP's forecast for the highest value in the block of 3 by 3 grid cells (4 km each) around the launch. AUSRASP publishes the updraft in whole metres per second, so Good needs 3 m/s9.8 ft/sec or more and Strong 4 m/s13.1 ft/sec or more. Where AUSRASP's figures are not available a block uses the page's own estimate from the global model, named in the line under the title; the estimate uses 2.5 m/s8.2 ft/sec and 4.0 m/s13.1 ft/sec.
  • Turbulence is not measured. It is inferred from wind strength, estimated gusts and storm energy. Wind shear (the difference between ground and upper wind) is shown in the details for information but does not change the grade.
  • Limitations. The grades come from computer models on a grid, not from measurements. It cannot see local ridge or valley effects, so check the live station chart and the Bureau of Meteorology warnings before you go. Days 5 to 7 use the same rules on rougher values.

How the numbers are calculated

Where the data comes from and how it is turned into thermal figures

Where the data comes from

  • Wind, gusts, rain, storms and temperature. NOAA GFS 0.25 degree (no regional model), run 2026-10-10T12Z. This is NOAA's GFS global weather model, published free by NOAA, on a grid of about 25 km. The page reads the grid point nearest the launch: wind at 10 m and at four pressure levels above it (interpolated to launch altitude), gusts, rain rate, storm energy (CAPE) and the temperature at 2 m. Nothing in the forecast is measured: it is all model output.
  • Thermal height and updraft. From AUSRASP's Victoria forecast, which runs a 4 km regional weather model (WRF) started from the same global model and works out thermal figures from it. The page reads two of AUSRASP's published values for the highest value in the block of 3 by 3 grid cells (4 km each) around the launch: thermalling height (the lowest of the height where the average updraft falls to 225 feet a minute, cumulus cloudbase and overdevelopment cloudbase, in metres above sea level) and the thermal updraft velocity (W*), which AUSRASP publishes in whole metres per second. Each block uses the AUSRASP hour valid at its start time. The runs behind this page started Sat 10 Oct 11:00 AEDT, Sat 10 Oct 23:00 AEDT; AUSRASP re-runs each day separately, and the page checks for new runs through the day. AUSRASP is not used for wind or rain. The days the line under the title says use the estimate are the ones AUSRASP could not supply (not available, incomplete or too old); they use the calculation below, and their thermal figures are marked est. The page shows AUSRASP's figures, not its maps; see them at ausrasp.com.
  • Today at Mystic. Current conditions and today's hourly forecast come from Open-Meteo, loaded by your browser. They are a model estimate for the launch, not a measurement.
  • The weather station chart. This is the one measured source: wind recorded at the launch by the club's station and shown by FreeFlightWx.
  • The grading limits. The wind bands and flyable direction come from the North East Victorian Hang Gliding Club's published Mystic site settings (set for an intermediate pilot on a low-end B paraglider).
  • Acknowledgement. AUSRASP's Victoria forecast is supported by the Victorian Hang Gliding and Paragliding Association (VHPA), and AUSRASP is run on donations.

How each thermal figure is worked out

Steps 2 to 4 are the page's own estimate from the global model. They are used only for blocks AUSRASP does not cover; blocks with AUSRASP figures take height and updraft from AUSRASP and use the other steps as written.

  1. Wind at launch (800 m2,625 ft). The model gives the wind at the surface and at fixed pressure levels. The page interpolates between them to the launch altitude, ignoring any level below the model's own ground.
  2. Heating at that hour. GFS reports the heat going into the air from the ground as an average since the last multiple of 6 hours. The page works out the value for each hour from two consecutive averages, so the late afternoon is not overstated and the morning not understated.
  3. Updraft strength. The convective velocity scale: the cube root of (gravity divided by air temperature) times the heating (per unit of air density and heat capacity) times the boundary layer depth. Stronger heating and a deeper layer both make stronger thermals. It is the average strength of a dry thermal; clouds can make real thermals stronger. It is zero when the ground is not heating the air.
  4. Thermal height. The height above sea level that usable thermals reach: launch height (800 m2,625 ft) plus 48% of the boundary layer depth, or launch height itself when the updraft is below 1.1 m/s3.8 ft/sec, the critical value for a usable thermal. It is lower than the top of the boundary layer, which is how high smoke would rise rather than how high a glider can climb. Heights are shown rounded down to the nearest 100 m (or 100 ft in feet) because the forecast is not that precise.
  5. Thermal quality. 100% at an updraft of 1.6 m/s5.2 ft/sec, in proportion below that, minus 4.6 points per knot2.5 points per km/h of wind above 6 kts12 kph (the stronger of the 10 m and 850 hPa winds), because wind breaks thermals up. Rounded to the nearest 10%. With AUSRASP's whole-number updraft, quality takes only a few values.
  6. Temperature at thermal height. The ground temperature less 9.8 °C for every kilometre climbed (the dry rate of cooling) from launch to the thermal height shown.
  7. Rain and storm energy. Taken directly from the model's value for that hour. The 10 m gust is also the model's value.
  8. Gusts at launch and at thermal height (estimates). No model publishes these, so they are rules of thumb. The gust at launch is the larger of the model's 10 m gust and the launch wind plus 50% of how much stronger the wind is at the top of the thermals, because thermals mix the faster upper air down to the ground. The gust at thermal height is the wind there times 1.25. The wind at the top of the thermals is AUSRASP's where it is available. The multipliers are starting values: they will be tuned against what pilots actually fly.
  9. Wind from AUSRASP. AUSRASP's regional model has a 4 km grid and often shows a stronger wind on the ridge than the global model. Where its 10 m wind is stronger than the global model's wind at launch, the block is graded on AUSRASP's, and the reasons say so.
  10. Sun. AUSRASP's share of possible sunshine reaching the ground (the average of the cells around the launch); a block it cannot supply uses the global model's low, middle and high cloud cover, where each layer stops a fixed share of the sun when it is overcast. Below 70% sun, thermal quality is scaled by the sun divided by 70.

Limitations

  • The wind, gust, rain and temperature figures come from a global model with a grid of about 25 km, which cannot see individual ridges, valleys or the real ground height at the launch, so winds there are usually lighter than the real thing. Check the station chart on the day.
  • The estimate's constants were fitted to a small comparison, so they are reasonable, not proven.
  • AUSRASP is an independent, volunteer-run service. Its figures are for 4 km grid cells over steep ground, so they can differ from what you find at the launch. The page takes the highest of the cells around the launch to get a better indication of the best thermal conditions over the mountain; the height and the updraft are each the highest in that block, which may be different cells. The page cannot tell you how old a run is beyond the time shown, and uses a run for at most 60 hours after it started.
  • These are forecasts of average conditions. Individual thermals vary widely around them.
